Whipping **mascarpone into a whipped cream** is a misleading way to put it, and it causes most of the failures: **mascarpone alone does not whip.** It thickens, then it curdles, then it splits. What people call mascarpone whipped cream is in fact a **blended** whipped cream, and everything comes down to the ratio.

I wasted three tubs of mascarpone before realising I was not getting the technique wrong, I was getting the recipe wrong.

## Why mascarpone alone curdles

Whipped cream holds because the fat globules in liquid cream clump around the air bubbles you whisk in. So you need globules that are **free in a liquid**.

Mascarpone is not a liquid: it is an already coagulated cream, around 40 % fat, whose structure is set. Whisking it does not create foam, it **breaks the existing emulsion**: the water separates and you get those shiny lumps.

Same logic as [whipped chocolate ganache](/en/blog/whipped-chocolate-ganache/): the fat must be organised before it is aerated, never during.

## The ratio, the only thing that really matters

| Mascarpone to cream ratio | Resulting texture | Best used for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | light, close to a classic whipped cream | quick topping, fruit cups |
| **1 to 2** | **firm and supple, pipes well** | **the reference ratio, good everywhere** |
| 1 to 1 | dense, almost buttery | layer cakes, maximum hold in summer |
| Mascarpone alone | curdles every time | nothing |

The reference ratio, the one I use nine times out of ten: **125 g of mascarpone to 250 g of double cream**, roughly one small tub and one carton.

The cream must be **full fat, at least 30 %**. A 15 % cream will not whip, whatever the mascarpone.

## The order of the steps, which is counter-intuitive

This is where nearly everyone goes wrong, myself included for two years.

1. **Everything must be cold**: bowl, whisk, cream, mascarpone. I put the bowl in the freezer ten minutes beforehand. Do not bring the mascarpone to room temperature, contrary to what many recipes say.
2. **Loosen the mascarpone first**, with a spatula, using only **two tablespoons of the cream**. You want a smooth paste with no lumps. No electric whisk at this stage.
3. **Pour the rest of the cold cream** onto that smooth base, and only then start whisking.
4. **Medium speed, not maximum.** A cream whipped slowly is more stable and forgives a few extra seconds.
5. **Icing sugar halfway**, once the cream starts to thicken. Caster sugar does not fully dissolve and leaves a gritty cream.

Allow two to three minutes. **Stop at soft peaks**: the cream falls back in a soft point on the lifted whisk. Beyond that it turns grainy, then to butter.

## Rescuing a split cream

As long as it has not turned properly buttery, it is usually recoverable.

**Stop the mixer immediately.** Add **one or two tablespoons of very cold cream** and fold it in **by hand**, with a whisk, without whipping: just stir. The cold cream rebuilds the emulsion and smooths the lumps.

If the fat has genuinely separated, the whipped cream is gone, but the mixture is not wasted: it goes into a [no-bake chocolate cake](/en/blog/no-bake-chocolate-cake/), where texture no longer matters.

## What this cream holds, and for how long

This is the real point of mascarpone, and the reason to prefer it to a plain whipped cream:

- **It does not weep.** A classic whipped cream releases liquid after a few hours, this one does not.
- **It holds for 48 hours in the fridge**, covered with cling film touching the surface, without collapsing.
- **It pipes well** without melting under the warmth of your hand.
- **It needs no stabiliser**, no gelatine, no commercial fixative.

That also makes it comfortable on a tart that has to wait, unlike a classic pastry cream such as the one in a [French custard tart](/en/blog/french-custard-tart/), which does not whip at all.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can mascarpone be whipped on its own?**
No. Mascarpone is an already coagulated cream with no free fat globules to aerate. Whisking it alone breaks its emulsion and produces lumps, then a water-fat separation. It has to be combined with full-fat liquid cream.

**What is the right mascarpone to cream ratio?**
One part mascarpone to two parts full-fat cream, so 125 g to 250 g, suits almost every use. One to three gives a lighter cream, one to one a dense cream that holds heat better but turns buttery quickly under the whisk.

**Why is my mascarpone whipped cream grainy?**
Either the mascarpone was whisked before being loosened with a little cream, or the whole thing was whipped too long or too fast. In both cases, adding a spoonful of very cold cream and stirring by hand with a whisk usually rescues the texture.

**How long does mascarpone whipped cream keep?**
Two days in the fridge, in a container with cling film touching the surface. It does not weep like a classic whipped cream, which makes it the most reliable cream for a cake prepared the day before.

**Icing sugar or caster sugar?**
Icing sugar, added once the cream starts to thicken. Caster sugar does not have time to dissolve in a cold preparation and leaves a gritty feel.
