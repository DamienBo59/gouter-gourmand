**Chocolate pastry cream** is not a vanilla cream with chocolate added. Chocolate contains cocoa butter, which sets as it cools, and cocoa solids, which thicken: added to a classic recipe, it gives a cream you could slice. **So the starch has to come down as the chocolate goes up.**

My first batch of chocolate eclairs had a filling so firm it cracked the choux pastry as I piped it in. The cream tasted good, it had simply been thickened twice.

## What chocolate changes in the cream

Pastry cream holds thanks to starch, which swells and gels at around 85 °C, trapping the water in the milk. It is the same mechanism as in a [French custard tart](/en/blog/french-custard-tart/), which is nothing more than pastry cream baked in the oven.

Chocolate adds a second structure on top:

- **cocoa butter**, solid at room temperature, which sets the cream as it cools;
- **cocoa solids**, which absorb water as a little flour would;
- **sugar**, already present in dark chocolate, which has to come out of the recipe.

The result: with the same amount of starch, chocolate pastry cream is noticeably firmer once cold than its vanilla version.

## Dosing, by use

| Dark chocolate per 500 ml milk | Cornflour | Texture once cold | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 g | 40 g | soft, light flavour | tarts, choux |
| **100 g** | **35 g** | **creamy, clearly chocolatey** | **eclairs, choux, tarts** |
| 150 g | 25 g | dense, almost a crémeux | tart bases, glasses |

Beyond 150 g you move away from pastry cream towards a crémeux, and the starch becomes almost unnecessary.

## The reference ratios

To fill 10 to 12 eclairs or a 24 cm tart:

- 500 ml whole milk
- 4 egg yolks
- 70 g sugar, instead of 100 g for a vanilla cream
- 35 g cornflour
- 100 g dark chocolate at 60 or 70 %, finely chopped
- 10 g unsweetened cocoa, optional, for a deeper colour
- 25 g butter

**Whole milk matters**, for the same reason as in the custard tart: the fat contributes to the texture, and a cream made with semi-skimmed milk is paler and more rubbery.

## The method

1. **Bring the milk to a simmer.**
2. **Mix the yolks with the sugar**, then the cornflour and cocoa, just until smooth. No need for long whisking until pale.
3. **Pour a third of the hot milk onto the yolks** while whisking, then return everything to the pan.
4. **Cook while whisking constantly**, scraping the bottom and corners, until it boils. **Let it boil for one full minute**: this finishes gelling the starch and removes the floury taste.
5. **Off the heat, add the chopped chocolate** and whisk until fully melted, then the butter.
6. **Transfer to a wide dish, cover with film touching the surface**, and cool quickly, ideally set over iced water, before it goes into the fridge.

The chocolate always goes in **off the heat**. In a boiling cream it can catch on the bottom and leave hard specks.

## Smoothing without breaking it

Cold, chocolate pastry cream sets into a block. Before piping, it needs loosening **with a whisk, for a few seconds, just until smooth again**.

This is where many people go wrong the other way: whisked for a long time, or with an electric mixer, cold pastry cream turns runny because the starch gel breaks. Once broken, it does not re-form.

For a lighter filling, fold in a third of whipped cream: that is the principle of crème diplomate. A cream whipped with mascarpone, like [mascarpone whipped cream](/en/blog/mascarpone-whipped-cream/), also works and holds better, which makes it a good option for a chocolate take on the [strawberry mascarpone tart](/en/blog/strawberry-mascarpone-tart/).

## What goes wrong, and why

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cream too firm, brittle | too much starch for the amount of chocolate | 35 g cornflour for 100 g chocolate |
| Cream stays runny | not boiled long enough | one full minute at a rolling boil |
| Lumps | yolks cooked by milk that was too hot, or a scorched bottom | add the milk in two goes, scrape constantly, pass through a sieve |
| Hard chocolate specks | chocolate added over the heat | always off the heat, finely chopped |
| Skin on the surface | film not touching the cream | film directly on the surface |

This is not a ganache: a [whipped chocolate ganache](/en/blog/whipped-chocolate-ganache/) is made from cream and chocolate alone, with no eggs or starch, and is meant to be whipped. Pastry cream never is.

## Storage

**48 hours in the fridge**, with film touching the surface, and no longer: it is an egg cream, rich in milk, and keeps poorly. It does not freeze, as the starch releases its water when thawed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can chocolate pastry cream be made with cocoa powder only?**
Yes, with 30 to 40 g of unsweetened cocoa per 500 ml of milk, keeping 100 g of sugar and 40 g of cornflour. The flavour is drier and more bitter, and the texture softer, since there is no cocoa butter to set it.

**Why is my chocolate pastry cream too thick?**
Because chocolate thickens it as it cools, on top of the starch. A vanilla recipe with chocolate added always gives a cream that is too firm. Cutting the cornflour by about a quarter solves it.

**Can I use milk chocolate?**
Yes, using 150 g per 500 ml of milk since it holds less cocoa, and bringing the sugar down to 40 g. The flavour is gentler and the cream slightly softer.

**How do I rescue a lumpy cream?**
Push it through a fine sieve while still hot, pressing with a spatula. If the lumps come from a bottom that caught, do not scrape the pan when pouring.
